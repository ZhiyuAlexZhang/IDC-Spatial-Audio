OVERALL ASSESSMENT

The draft has stronger empirical material than its headings and presentation currently
communicate. It already contains substantive interpretations: hearing a difference does
not necessarily change appreciation; separating voices also changes felt proximity;
recording at the mouth differs from recording a listening perspective; and the same
vibration can be welcome for passengers but questionable for drivers. These are more
informative than an application list. The next revision should foreground and substantiate
these relationships, while retaining the evidence that complicates them.

The comparison does NOT support two categorical rules: remove all design tables, or turn
every heading into a declarative claim. Relevant papers use multiple formats. Their better
passages explain relationships among observations, reasons, contexts and consequences.
Some make that meaning visible in headings; others develop it in subsection openings,
interaction sequences, contrasts, closing analyses, or Discussion models.

My recommendation: keep a thematic account; greatly simplify the large team table;
present selected designs individually inside the themes; sharpen the analytical work,
especially the second part of 4.1 and the classification in 4.3. Do not replace the whole
section with six design descriptions. A modest change in architecture plus stronger
paragraphs is more justified than another complete reorganization.

1. SHOULD THE TABLE OF DESIGNS GO?

The user is right about the current table's burden, but an individual narrative for every
design is not automatically the stronger alternative.

A direct counterexample is Newman et al., Purposeful Play (ISMIR 2024), an unusually useful
match because it involves children's evaluation and co-design of music applications in two
short sessions. Table 2 on PDFp3 summarizes eight child-designed apps in three columns:
child, app name, description. Figure 1 onp4 then shows one design, Dreamer. Findingspp3-5
analyze patterns across the designs and earlier app experiences. The table provides a map;
the selected figure makes an example inspectable; the thematic prose explains meaning.
This is not IDC, so it demonstrates a viable matched approach rather than an IDC mandate.

Woodward et al., AR Headsets (CHI 2022), uses selected low-tech artifacts (Figures1 and3,
PDFpp5 and7) and a conceptual model (Figure 2,p 6), without cataloguing every final design.
Dangol et al., AI Toys (IDC 2026), refers to individual participant comics within a thematic
account (section 4.4,p 10; Figure 5,p 11), with fuller comics in an appendix. Hunt et al.,
Designing Together (IDC 2023), uses selected interaction vignettes and an appendix of
sessions. These are different solutions to different evidentiary problems.

Absence of design inventories in the Instagram studies is weak evidence against your
inventory: those studies did not generate a comparable set of six final co-design boards.
Likewise, session, demographic, codebook, and outcome tables are not design tables. They
must not be counted together to claim that this community prefers or avoids tables.

Your table (draft lines59-80) currently combines at least four jobs:
- orienting the reader to six teams and their concepts;
- describing format and prompts;
- cataloguing almost every feature and alternative;
- documenting adult/teen origins, drawing roles, timestamps and attribution uncertainty.

That makes it closer to a compressed audit record than a useful reader overview. Much of
its most important content is repeated in the prose. The generic six-board placeholder
(lines49-57) adds no readable evidence yet. Neither issue is solved by eliminating tables
as a format.

Recommended presentation:
- If needed, retain a very small overview: team; concept/intended activity; distinctive
  spatial feature. A short provenance note can flag mixed authorship. Put format/prompt
  details in Methods and the detailed origin/feature record in an appendix or supplement.
- Show selected original artifact crops alongside the paragraphs that interpret them:
  ear versus mouth microphones in the hoodie; positions at the virtual table; driver
  versus passenger placement in the car. The practice app can be a fourth example if
  its room-acoustics choices cannot be explained clearly in prose.
- Preserve handwritten/typed evidence where legible. Label any cleaned redraw as a
  researcher reconstruction, retaining the original in the supplement.
- Give each caption an evidentiary purpose: what feature the reader should inspect,
  whose design it is, and what the prose uses it to establish. Avoid just “six boards.”
- Do not give all six concepts equal narrative space. Select for analytical contribution,
  a useful contrast, or a challenging case. Keep the full corpus discoverable elsewhere.

If the annotated figures and a short orientation paragraph supply all necessary navigation,
there is no obligation to retain even the small table. The recommendation is functional,
not a requirement for a table-plus-figure formula.

2. ARE THE THEMES ONLY CATEGORIES?

Some of the headings are weak, but “category” does not mean “meaningless.” A descriptive
heading can locate a substantial finding. Nor does a claim-shaped heading guarantee
analysis. The problem is whether the reader learns a pattern, relationship or distinction
that goes beyond the topic and the quotations themselves.

Actual comparators:

- Yip et al., Money (IDC 2023), uses descriptive headings such as “The modality of money.”
  Each thematic section closes with an explicit summary analysis (section 4,PDFpp5-8).
  It does not require every heading to be a sentence claiming an effect.
- Rubegni et al., Robots (IDC 2022), uses broad attribute categories but pairs opposing
  beliefs and the hopes/fears associated with them (section 4,pp 5-8). The analytical
  contribution lies in that ambivalence and relation, not simply the category names.
- Landesman et al., Instagram (IDC 2024), combines RQ headings with both topical labels
  and interpretive ones. “Boredom In, Boredom Out” (4.1.2,p 6) explains boredom as entry,
  ongoing experience and exit trigger. “Moments of Connection” is more descriptive.
  The prose connects the two; Discussion develops the interaction cycle (Figure 3,p 10).
- Dangol et al., AI Toys (IDC 2026), has broad headings about sensemaking and interaction,
  then more interpretive subheadings about repair and abandonment (section 4,pp 7-10).
  Its strongest passages trace changing actions and expectations within episodes.
- Landesman et al., Teen AI (IJCCI 2026), groups findings by contexts and levels of concern
  (section 4,pp 6-10), while interpreting central/supporting tasks and cognitive effort.
  The two-axis synthesis appears in Discussion (5.1,pp 10-11). It does not force every
  context into a separate novel theoretical construct.

A reviewer-facing distinction: findings should interpret the empirical material; Discussion
can connect those interpretations to prior literature, wider implications and a more general
model. Keeping interpretation out of Findings would make this draft weaker. Demanding the
entire theoretical contribution in every Findings heading would also be excessive.

APPLICATION TO EACH PART OF THE CURRENT DRAFT

4.1 Making Sense of the Presentation (lines82-100)

This is a defensible umbrella, but it names an activity more than an insight. The prose
already has a more useful argument: locating the effect took work and involved the visual
presentation; noticing the effect did not settle its value. Make those distinctions visible.

“Working to hear a difference” is already a meaningful process heading. Retain it or refine
it lightly. “What the difference did, and what teens brought to it” is substantially weaker:
it bundles attention to musical components, enjoyment, prior gaming/headphone experience,
familiarity, and expectations of concerts. A reader cannot anticipate the finding.

A more informative heading is “Hearing more detail did not necessarily change appreciation.”
Use the contrasting familiarity accounts to establish that distinction. Prior experience
then explains how participants judged the demonstration; it need not become a separate
subtheme unless there is enough analysis to support one. A short final paragraph can say
that the demonstration was interpreted against existing experiences of games, concerts and
8D audio, rather than treated as a completely unfamiliar technology.

Do not turn the audiovisual episode into proof that visuals caused perception. The current
facilitator sequence is important because reassurance changed the conversational context.
The useful claim concerns how participants articulated the experience under these study
conditions. It is not a sensory-isolation or learning result.

4.2 Putting Spatial Relationships to Use (lines102-129)

This is the strongest part. Its purposes are legitimate organizing categories, but the
writer must keep explaining what is distinctively spatial and what that enables in the
participants' reasoning. The alternative is a feature wishlist organized into three bins.

The FaceTime passage should distinguish the proposal's anticipated benefit from a tested
one: Nadia expected separation to help communication and also remarked on the unfamiliar
proximity. “Weird” can express ambivalence or novelty; it does not alone establish intrusion,
rejection, or a privacy concern. Do not force her into the “intrusive” category later.

The hoodie episode is strongest when it explains the contrast between capturing a voice
and capturing how an event was heard. That is more informative than saying teens wanted
to make/keep music. Its boundedness matters: this developed within one team, partly from
adult facilitation. A rich single case can be valuable without becoming a pervasive cohort
preference. Fix the visibly broken concert quotation before relying on it.

The virtual table is strongest when spatial placement becomes a social choice. Seat-claiming,
muting and timeout raise questions about whose choice changes whose listening. The artifact
and conversation did not specify enough interaction rules to show actual exclusion,
negotiation, conflict, or a demonstrated gain in agency. Frame these as possibilities and
unresolved implications of the concepts.

For each purpose, use one rich episode plus one supporting or contrasting case. This is
already partly achieved. The revision should strengthen the connections rather than add
more examples indiscriminately. “Table 2 lists the rest” currently implies exhaustive
coverage; confirm it or use narrower wording.

4.3 Deciding When It Is Worth It (lines131-150)

The top heading is useful, but desirable/unnecessary/intrusive is a less convincing analytic
partition than the actual evidence. Payment is not a form of intrusion. Wanting a choice is
not the same as wanting the effect; finding a proposal interesting is not endorsement of
its implementation. A single participant can occupy multiple positions, which the draft
acknowledges but does not yet resolve structurally.

Organize the material around conditions, not fixed participant positions. Three workable
local groupings are:
- fitting existing practices and equipment (including leaving satisfactory practices alone);
- placing or limiting the effect for a particular listener/activity;
- qualifying access and payment.

These are organizing options, not claims that must become three equally long subsections.
The passenger/driver case is strong enough to carry a main analytic paragraph; payment can
be a short qualification if less developed. Privacy is thin and intertwined with playful
premium features, so it should not become a large standalone theme without further evidence.

The supported interpretation is that valuation depends on what a spatial feature does in a
situation and who experiences its consequences. This can include refusing unnecessary change,
not merely fine-tuning an otherwise desirable upgrade. Separate this broader design judgment
from 4.1's narrower distinction between noticing a demonstration and appreciating the song.

3. OTHER IMPORTANT DIFFERENCES FROM THE BETTER COMPARATOR PASSAGES

A. Make the analytical relationship explicit before asking quotations to carry it.

Several paragraphs already do this. The draft needs consistency, not an arbitrary quote
quota. A productive paragraph moves from a bounded proposition to an inspectable episode,
explains the relevant detail, then compares a case or exception. A closing sentence should
add meaning rather than paraphrase the quote or announce “This answers RQ1/RQ2.”

Footprint (IDC 2026,5.1.3,pp 9-10) provides a useful model: two groups connect answer length
with resource cost but give different explanations. The shared feature is not itself the
finding; the reasoning differentiates the cases. Your analogous question is why similar
spatial arrangements matter differently in communication, musical practice and social play.

B. Keep youth contributions specific; do not manufacture empowerment.

Mediating Culture (DIS 2024,4.3,pp 11-12) and AI Toys show adults, peers and research materials
shaping the encounter. Your attention to adult origins is a strength. Keep the crucial
origin and response within the episode, but move repeated general qualification into Methods
and detailed audit information into an appendix. This should make the prose clearer while
preserving provenance, not erase adult influence to make the contribution sound stronger.

Describe the choice sought: selecting a listening position, moving sources, choosing a
recording perspective, or limiting the effect. These are evidence-backed proposals.
“Agency,” “ownership” and “empowerment” need an explicit meaning and evidence beyond the
presence of a switch. No deployed system or demonstrated change in these constructs exists
in this study. A youth-centered account can be valuable without claiming such outcomes.

C. Let play, jokes and refusal remain substantive.

Do not justify every idea by productivity, musical improvement or serious communication.
The prank, seat claim, Wrapped and social-gimmick episodes show that spatial relationships
can be material for play. At the same time, humorous premium features do not establish
stable willingness to pay or a worked-out governance model. The contextual detail matters.
The participants who already like their listening practices also contribute to what youth
want; they should not be treated as failed adopters or merely counterweights to enthusiasm.

D. Use the artifacts as evidence, not decoration.

Selected visuals in AR Headsets, AI Toys and Teen AI are tied to claims. Your actual boards
are still a placeholder in this file. Reading the original marks can help distinguish what
was drawn, what was spoken, and what the authors infer. A gallery of six illegible thumbnails
would offer less than three readable crops, linked to intact originals in the supplement.
This is a substantive presentation gap, not a cosmetic one.

E. Preserve the distinction between experienced, reported and imagined.

Purrble (CHI 2022,sections 4-5,pp 9-17) separates deployment accounts from workshop suggestions;
AI Toys separates enacted interaction from imagined futures. Your opening already makes
this distinction. Maintain it locally where readers might infer an outcome: the call would
not be shown to improve communication, the hoodie did not preserve memories in use, and the
car features were not evaluated for safety. General wording belongs in Methods; local verbs
such as proposed, expected, imagined and questioned often provide the needed precision.

F. Do not confuse a compelling local case with a general youth preference.

Some comparators offer longitudinal or larger deployed evidence. Their strength cannot be
borrowed simply by imitating their heading or conceptual model. Your workshop supports a
situated account of what these adolescents proposed and reasoned about. A one-team case
may deserve space because it reveals a distinction, while broader claims need evidence
across the relevant corpus. Frequency of features is not automatically analytical importance.

G. Finish the credibility work before polishing the pitch.

The draft still contains red VERIFY markers, uncertain speakers, an apparently malformed
concert quote and an unverified sticky-to-participant assumption. Resolve these against
the records or remove/narrow the dependent claims. Ensure the Methods explain the listening
setup, demonstration, prompts, facilitation and analysis. The comparison papers do not
remove this obligation, and a stronger story cannot compensate for unstable quotations.
This report has not independently re-audited every source timestamp.

4. A PRACTICAL REVISION TARGET

Keep the overall movement from encountering the effect, to proposing uses, to qualifying
its place. Make the three central empirical propositions clear early:

1. Noticing a spatial difference did not necessarily change appreciation; the demonstration
   was interpreted through visual cues, comparison and existing listening experiences.
2. In their proposals, teens used spatial arrangements to shape attention, listening
   perspective and social presence, with different purposes attached to similar choices.
3. The value they anticipated depended on the activity, listener and conditions of use;
   some qualified or declined the change altogether.

These propositions are working interpretations to test against the full evidence, not
new discoveries established by this literature comparison. The current data excerpts
support parts more strongly than others. Do not imply each participant or design supports
all three.

Possible broad headings (optional wording, not a required framework):
4.1 Hearing a Difference Did Not Necessarily Change Appreciation
4.2 Shaping What Is Heard, From Where, and With Whom
4.3 Qualifying Where Spatial Effects Belong

Keep descriptive subheadings where they help navigation. Give “Making and keeping music”
a specific opening about voice versus listening perspective; retain “Being somewhere, with
someone” if the prose explains what being-with adds to immersion. Avoid another wholesale
reorganization before these claims and evidence links are secure.

The likely contribution is an account of the spatial choices adolescents proposed, the
purposes they attached to those choices, and the conditions they raised around them.
That is a credible direction for IDC. It is more specific than “teens want control” and
more defensible than claiming that spatial audio has historically only been playback or
that this workshop demonstrated empowerment. Establish the domain gap separately through
spatial-audio literature; these CDY comparators cannot establish it for you.

WHAT THIS CORPUS DOES AND DOES NOT ESTABLISH

It establishes several viable published ways to organize evidence and interpretation.
It does not establish a single IDC house style, the reviewers' reasons for accepting any
paper, or an acceptance probability for this manuscript. Published examples themselves
vary in analytical quality. Borrow the move that fits the evidence, not their authority.

Priority reading: Purposeful Play for overview-plus-selected-artifact presentation;
Instagram IDC 2024 for relationships across themes; Money IDC 2023 for summary analysis;
AI Toys IDC 2026 for interaction sequences and imagined futures; Teen AI IJCCI 2026 for
adolescent co-design and contextual valuation. AR Headsets CHI 2022 is the closest immersive
technology comparator. Detailed notes cover all 18 papers.
