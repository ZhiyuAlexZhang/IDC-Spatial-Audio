ABSTRACT
Prior research indicates that many adolescents struggle to use social media intentionally, with negative impacts on well-being. This
presents an important but under-researched opportunity to improve youth well-being through interaction design. We developed
Locus, a mobile application that shapes adolescents’ entry experiences into the social media apps on their phones by prompting
them to reflect on their social media intentions before they open
an app and at the end of the day. In designing Locus, we combined
psychology theories—to identify design targets—with co-design
approaches—to design around these targets for youth’s lived experiences. Following co-design work with nine teens (M age =15.7 years),
we conducted an open trial field deployment (N = 54; M age =16.2
years) to explore how Locus was used and experienced by adolescents during their daily lives. Results provide preliminary evidence
that in-the-moment interventions for self-regulation skills can support adolescents’ intentional social media use.

INTRODUCTION

As many as 25% of adolescents engage in problematic social media
use (PSMU) [49, 72], characterized by difficulty controlling social
media behavior, with negative impacts on sleep, academic performance, relationships, and well-being [3]. Even among adolescents
whose behavior does not meet PSMU criteria, prior work indicates
that it is common for youth to approach their social media use
in an unintentional way, mindlessly scrolling through their feeds,
allowing auto-play to show them video after video, and reacting
to rather than interacting with the people and content they encounter online [20, 81]. Youth report experiencing these practices
as unsatisfying but say they struggle to break their habits [20, 81].
Unintentional social media use is associated with lowered levels of
subjective well-being among youth [17, 60].
We developed Locus,1 a mobile application that shapes adolescents’ entry experiences into the social media apps on their phones
by prompting them to reflect on their social media intentions before they start a session. Locus also delivers end-of-day reflection
prompts, sent as push notifications to a user’s home screen, that
encourage adolescents to reflect on their social media use that day

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Well-Being, Agency, and Self-Regulation in
Adolescents’ Social Media Use
Although many adolescents derive benefits from their social media
engagement (e.g., [17, 19, 20, 66, 81]), as many as 25% experience
problematic social media use (PSMU) [3, 5, 17, 37, 49, 72]. PSMU
is sometimes encompassed within the more general construct of
problematic internet use (PIU) and is related to problematic smartphone use (PSU) [3]. Core to definitions of PSMU, PIU, and PSU
are difficulties controlling one’s behavior in relation to technology
use, with negative impacts on sleep quality, relationships, academic
performance, and personal well-being [3, 24, 37, 49].
Even among those adolescents whose social media use does not
meet PSMU criteria, many youth nevertheless struggle to maintain
a sense of purpose and intention when they embark on a social
media session. Weinstein and James [81] documented the many
pulls on adolescents’ attention, including alluring personalized content, infinite scroll, the unpredictable rewards and social validation
associated with features such as the like button and comments,
and the “quicksand” of social media-based social comparisons (see
also [54–56]). Adolescents who fall down these social media “rabbit holes” are more likely to report lowered subjective well-being
[56, 60].
Although people of all ages experience agency-undermining
experiences with social media (e.g., [42, 78]), adolescents may find
it particularly difficult to achieve a sense of personal agency on
TikTok, Snapchat, and other social media platforms. This is due to
the gap between adolescents’ heightened emotional responses and
their lagging self-regulation skills. Compared to children and adults,
adolescents display an increased sensitivity to social situations and a
greater tendency toward emotional reactivity when confronted with
such situations [74, 75]. At the same time, self-regulation skills such
as reflection, planning, goal-setting, and self-monitoring [4, 7, 51]
are relatively under-developed during this developmental period
[9, 10]. Indeed, Van Deursen et al. [22] found that adolescents were
more likely than adults to have trouble engaging in self-regulation
behaviors when using their smartphones.

323

To summarize, the fundamentally social nature of social media,
combined with persuasive designs such as infinite scroll and notifications, regularly place adolescents in situations where their
tendency toward emotional reactivity overwhelms their still developing self-regulation abilities [20]. This body of work points to the
value of intervening in adolescents’ social media use at the moment
when self-regulation skills are most needed and least likely to be
used.

2.2

Interventions to Support Digital Agency and
Well-Being

Smartphone overuse is a prevalent target of HCI interventions that
focus on designing for personal agency and well-being. MyTime
[29] supports people in setting and achieving goals related to the
time they spend on their smartphones. TypeOut [84] combines a
typing-based unlock process with self-affirmation content to support users in reducing smartphone overuse. Both interventions
target smartphone overuse by supporting people’s sense of personal agency. Other interventions targeting smartphone reduction
similarly focus on promoting personal agency and well-being (e.g.,
[33, 38, 40, 57]).
Increasingly, interventions are targeting the quality of phone
use in addition to or instead of quantity of use as a core outcome
(recognizing that these two dimensions are often entwined). For
instance, Chirp [86] is a mobile Twitter client that researchers developed to increase users’ sense of agency during their Twitter
use. MindPhone [77] draws on mindfulness techniques to target absentminded smartphone. The intervention’s focus on goal-setting
and reflection align it with other interventions that target selfregulation skills as a mechanism for supporting personal agency
in phone use (e.g., [34, 39, 45, 48]). In the current study, we build
on this work by focusing on self-regulation skills as the primary
mechanism for promoting adolescents’ sense of agency in their
phone-based social media use.
When considered alongside theoretical work (e.g., [43, 45]) and
empirical investigations of commercial technologies (e.g., [16, 46,
47]), existing interventions point to some overarching takeaways for
future research focused on designing for personal agency and wellbeing. Overly restrictive techniques such as lockout mechanisms,
while sometimes effective, can lead to user frustration and even an
increase in phone usage [35, 46, 47]. Notification messages and goalsetting features are less restrictive, but some researchers caution
they can be easily dismissed [41, 84]. Lyngs et al. [46] propose a
“Goldilocks” level of support for digital self-control interventions
that target self-regulation. Such design mechanisms should adapt
to users’ personal goals and usage patterns and provide enough
support to encourage behavior change without being so restrictive
that users abandon the intervention altogether.
A notable gap in this research base is the scarcity of interventions
that are designed from the beginning with adolescent users in mind
[28, 68]. In their recent meta-analysis of digital self-control tools,
Roffarello and De Russis found that only two of the 64 studies
they reviewed included youth of any age; the vast majority of
studies involve university students and office workers. Outside of
HCI, teen-focused interventions have been developed that target
self-regulation in the context of problematic technology use (e.g.,

Within HCI, research is starting to explore interaction designs

to support adolescents’ self-regulation behaviors around their social media use by asking study participants to record and send
researchers daily reflections about their social media use. However,
because this intervention centered daily communication with a
researcher, it did not function as a standalone intervention that is
embedded directly into adolescents’ daily social media use. There
is a need to develop and test intervention mechanisms that make
use of mobile technologies to reach adolescents where they are and
when they are most in need of intervention [1, 28, 52, 71].

3 LOCUS APP DESIGN PROCESS
3.1 Identifying Plausible Intervention Targets
The first step in our design process was to draw on a combination of
psychology and adolescent social media use literatures to identity
relevant psychological constructs that we could target. In what
follows, we discuss the theory and prior empirical work that led us
to focus on two specific factors: (1) youth self-regulation skills and
(2) in-the-moment self-monitoring and goal setting.
3.1.1 Targeting Self-Regulation Skills. As discussed in Section 2.1,
prior work has shown that many teens routinely experience a lack
of personal agency in their social media use [20, 60, 81]. Teens who
experience low levels of social media agency are more likely to
report lowered subjective well-being [60]. This finding is consistent with self-determination theory (SDT), which underscores the
relationship between personal agency (also referred to as autonomy) and well-being [21, 70]. Therefore, our overarching goal in
this work is to support adolescents’ sense of agency in their social
media experiences.
A sub-theory of SDT is organismic integration theory (OIT),
which presents a continuum of motivation and regulated behavior
[14]. According to OIT, the more self-regulated a behavior is, the
more self-determined it is felt to be. OIT highlights the central
role that self-regulation plays in promoting a sense of autonomy
and agency in one’s actions. Other theories similarly portray selfregulation as the basis for purposeful action and a key ingredient in

324

personal well-being [4, 7, 51]. Recognizing this connection between
self-regulation and well-being, other HCI researchers encourage
the use of design mechanisms that target self-regulation skills when
designing interventions to support digital well-being ([45]).
As discussed in Section 2.1, self-regulation is a particularly suitable focus for our target population of adolescents in light of the developmental challenges that adolescents face in self-regulating their
behavior [51]. Prior research implicates self-regulation challenges
in adolescents’ problematic social media use (PSMU) [3, 5, 22, 37],
in addition to more benign social media experiences that may not
rise to the level of PSMU but are nevertheless experienced by adolescents as unsatisfying [60, 81].
Therefore, our first aim was to envision designs that would target
the self-regulation skills that adolescents are most likely to disregard
when faced with an immediately gratifying social media stimulus.
3.1.2 In-the-Moment Self-Monitoring and Goal-Setting Intervention.
In describing his social cognitive theory of self-regulation, Bandura
[4] observed that self-monitoring close in time to the target behavior
is key to developing a person’s self-regulation skills. This perspective is consistent with digital self-control interventions inspired by
dual-process theories of cognition [47, 84]. Dual-process theories
distinguish between two types of cognitive processing: Type 1 processes are intuitive, unconscious, and automatic, whereas Type 2
processes are reflective, conscious, and controlled [25]. Technology
interventions that seek to change people’s behavior target Type
2 processes at the moment when Type 1 processes are typically
dominant. In the case of smartphone overuse, for instance, justin-time interventions [53] might present notifications, reminders,
or prompts as a person is using their phone in an effort to stimulate Type 2 processes of cognition and prevent phone overuse (see
[47, 84]).
Based on this theoretical work, our second aim was to target Type
2 processes of cognition by envisioning approaches to prompt adolescents’ self-monitoring and goal setting prior to opening a social
media app on their phone. Our goal is to help adolescents reframe
the ensuing social media experience so that it feels intentional and
purposeful rather than reflexive and devoid of meaning.

3.2

Design of the Preliminary App Prototype

Drawing on the theories outlined in Section 3.1, we identified a set
of interaction design choices for the design of the preliminary Locus
app prototype. First, we designed Locus as a wrapper application
that adolescents use to open the social media apps on their phone.
This design choice allows us to shape users’ entry experiences into
their social media apps.
Second, we generated an initial set of app-entry prompts that appear the first time a teen opens a social media app on any given day.
Before being taken to their desired app, adolescents are prompted
to identify and reflect on their goals and intentions for their social
media session. In this way, the app-entry prompts encourage adolescents to engage Type 2 processes of cognition at the moment
when their Type 1 processes are typically strongest. Beyond Type
2 engagement, the prompts are aimed at helping adolescents to
frame the ensuing social media experience in personally meaningful terms.

Third, we identified a set of general end-of-day prompts that
encourage users to reflect on and self-monitor their social media
use during the course of the day, as well as identify strategies and
set goals for the following day. These prompts are not designed
specifically to override Type 1 processes in-the-moment, but rather
to help adolescents direct their Type 2 processes toward their social
media use by engaging and exercising key self-regulation skills.
For both types of prompt (app-entry and general end-of-day),
users can respond either through text entry or voice recording.
They can also choose to dismiss the prompt altogether and proceed
directly to their intended app. Drawing on prior research [46, 84],
we made these design choices in an effort to strike a balance between being overly restrictive (a danger in requiring a response)
and not engaging enough (a danger in prompts that do not invite
a user response). Even if an adolescent chooses not to record a
response, they will still see the prompt and must actively dismiss it
in order to enter the desired social media app. Thus, adolescents
are still receiving the intervention even if they do not record a
response.
Figure 1 presents the theory of change associated with our intervention. We hypothesize that the interaction techniques described
in this section (intervention) will support adolescents’ social mediarelated self-regulation behaviors (mechanism of change), leading to
a decrease in habitual use and—over time—an associated increased
sense of control and agency in their social media use (proximal outcome). Because of the links between meaningful technology use and
well-being [17, 42, 60], we would expect to see eventual increases
in adolescents’ personal well-being, such as lowered depressive
symptoms and anxiety levels, if used consistently over a longer
period (distal outcome).

3.3

Co-Design with Teens to Update and
Finalize the Prototype

We conducted virtual co-design sessions with nine teens in summer
2021 following the initial design and development of the prototype
app. The goals for these sessions were to: (1) gain initial insight
into how Locus might be taken up in the context of teens’ daily
social media use; (2) solicit feedback on the app’s core features; and
(3) identify and fix bugs in the app to improve its usability.

325

Following approval from our university’s institutional review
board, we recruited teen participants through word of mouth, social
media announcements, and our university’s research-participant
pool. We divided the nine participants we recruited into two groups.
The first group included four teens aged 14-16 years (M age = 15.0
years; 2 male, 1 female, 1 non-binary; 2 Asian, 2 white). The second
group included five teens aged 15-18 years (M age = 16.2 years; 3
male, 2 female; 3 Asian, 2 white).
Both groups of participants took part in three co-design sessions
each lasting 2.5 hours. These sessions were structured similarly following best practices for co-designing with teens [6, 31, 65, 85]. All
sessions took place via Zoom and used Miro boards to engage participants in different forms of hands-on ideation. The sessions were
led by three PhD student researchers with extensive experience in
co-design and working with teens in a participatory manner.
We used teen participants’ feedback to update the app’s prompts
before deployment. In particular, we attempted to use a more casual,
colloquial tone that would be appealing to teens, and we varied
the phrasing of the prompts (e.g., by using a mix of questions and
statements).
Teens provided inconsistent feedback on the timing of the
prompts. Some teens wanted greater frequency, whereas others
wanted less. Some teens appreciated receiving the questions before
entering a social media app, whereas others were frustrated by
having to delay their entry. We decided on one app-entry prompt
per app per day and one general end-of-day reflection prompt, with
the goal of encouraging teens to engage in consistent reflection
while not slowing them down so much that they become annoyed
and stop using Locus altogether.
During user testing, we learned that most teen participants entered social media platforms directly from the notifications that
appeared on their phone’s home screen. As a result, these teens
rarely entered their social media apps through Locus and therefore
never encountered the app-entry prompts. We addressed this issue
by programming Locus to control all social media notifications so
that clicking on a notification would take the user to Locus first
(see Figure 2A) instead of directly to the desired social media app.
Additional bugs that we identified and fixed included incorrectlytimed notifications and recurring notifications that would not go
away, a misaligned text box and questions, and lags in loading the
prompts and social media apps.

3.4

Final Locus Application Taken to Field
Deployment

Locus is a mobile application available on the Google Play Store for
Android devices with an Android version above 7.0 (Nougat). It is
designed as a wrapper application that allows users to open social
media apps directly through the Locus app. After opening Locus
on their phone, users see all of their social media apps displayed
together (see Figure 2A). When they select an app such as TikTok
or Snapchat, they are shown a text-based reflective prompt before
being taken to the desired app (see Figure 2B). Entering a response
is optional and can be done via text or speech recording. The appentry prompts are programmed to appear upon the first daily use
of a social media app. Users see one prompt per day per app. Locus
also sends a general prompt in the form of a notification once per
day at 9pm (see Figure 2C).
The backend of Locus consists of a Python server that provides
the list of prompts and stores users’ responses to prompts. This
means that the prompts can be changed at any time without having
to update Locus on users’ phones. In addition, the server authenticates registered users with an email and password combination and
hosts a website with instructions on how to install and use Locus.

RQ1: How do teens interact with the Locus app features over
the course of the two-week deployment period?
RQ2: To what extent does Locus engage teens in goal setting,
self-monitoring, and other self-regulation behaviors related to their
social media use?
RQ3: Does using the Locus app help teens experience greater
self-control and agency in their social media use?

OPEN TRIAL FIELD DEPLOYMENT

We conducted an open trial field deployment (N =54) to examine
the acceptability of Locus and how the app was taken up and experienced by adolescents in their everyday lives. We sought to answer
the following research questions:

Participants

Following approval from our university’s institutional review board,
we recruited English-speaking participants from the United States
through a paid Instagram ad that included a link to an initial
screener survey. To be included in the study, prospective participants had to live in the Unites States, own and use an Android
phone, be between the ages of 14 and 18, have a reliable internet
connection, and express an interest in changing their approach to

Sixty-six participants met the inclusion criteria and successfully
installed the Locus app on their phone. Of those participants, 54

interview (see Section 4.2). Table 1 includes a summary of participant demographics.

The pre-deployment survey asked questions about participants’
social media use, including which apps they used most frequently,
how they felt about their use of these apps, and how they felt about
their social media use more generally. In addition, we included
three validated scales measuring self-control and agency in social

Brief Self-Control Scale (BSCS) [76] to measure self-control
ability in the context of adolescents’ social media use (e.g.,
“In the past week, I was good at resisting temptation on social
media”). Participants used a 5-point Likert scale to indicate
how much each of the 13 statements reflected their experience using social media in the past week (1=not at all; 5=very
much). The internal consistency of the scale was satisfactory
(Cronbach’s alpha =0.77).
• Absentminded social media use. We adapted the 10item Smartphone Use Questionnaire-Absentminded (SUQ-A),
which measures perceptions of absentminded smartphone
use [50], to measure participants’ levels of absentminded
social media use. We chose absentmindedness because of its
opposition to intentionality, hypothesizing that the Locus
app would decrease participants’ absentminded social media
use as they became more intentional in their approach to social media. Participants used a 7-point Likert scale to indicate
how frequently they had the experiences identified in each
statement (e.g., “How often do you check social media out of
habit?”) on a typical day (1=never; 7=always). The internal
consistency of the scale was satisfactory (Cronbach’s alpha
=0.89).

The post-deployment survey mirrored the questions from the predeployment survey, with the addition of targeted questions about
participants’ experiences using the Locus app, how consistently
they used it, and any bugs they encountered. To assess the app’s selfperceived impact on social media use, participants were asked how
strongly they agreed or disagreed with the following statements:
“The Locus app helped me to think about my goals for using social
media” and “The Locus app made me feel more in control of my social
media use.” Response choices were on a Likert scale from 1 (strongly
disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
At the end of the two weeks, 26 participants took part in a
follow-up interview. Interview questions included both user experience questions and questions about the Locus app’s self-perceived
impact on participants’ approach to social media. The interviews
gave us a qualitative understanding of how teens appropriated and
experienced Locus and implications for further redesign.
To analyze the interviews, we used a reflexive thematic analysis
(TA) approach that emphasizes researcher reflexivity and interpretive depth (in contrast to post-positivist approaches that rely
on inter-coder agreement) [11–13]. Drawing on theory and prior
research (see Section 2), and guided by our three research questions, the first author read through the interview transcripts and
interviewer notes multiple times, highlighting and grouping ideas
that addressed each research question. For every highlighted idea
and across each interview transcript, the researcher recorded instances of both confirming and disconfirming evidence. The first
and third authors used this analysis to frame a series of interpretive
conversations and construct themes.

RESULTS

The results of the deployment study are organized around our three
research questions, which address teens’ interactions with the Locus app’s features (RQ1); engagement in self-regulation behaviors
(e.g., goal setting, reflection, self-monitoring) (RQ2); and perceived
changes in teens’ experiences of self-control and agency in their
social media use (RQ3). To protect participants’ privacy, we use
numerical participant identifiers in place of actual names.

5.1

RQ1 – Participants’ Interactions with the
Locus App’s Features (Acceptability)

Our first research question focused on the acceptability of the Locus
app to adolescent users. In designing Locus as a wrapper application,
we were initially concerned that teens would find it cumbersome
or annoying to hide the social media icons on their phones and
enter their desired apps instead through Locus. Although some
participants said they had to remind themselves initially to go
through Locus, participants said they adapted quickly and found
it straightforward to access their social media apps in this way.
80% of participants reported that they opened their social media
apps through the Locus app most of the time or all of the time
during the course of the two-week study. Of the 20% who used
Locus less regularly, they usually said they either forgot or found
it easier to access their social media apps directly instead of being
slowed down by an intermediary app. P51 (male, age 16) explained:
“Sometimes if I really needed to get to an app, I just kind of went to
that directly, because it was just easier to do that in the moment.”
We asked participants to reflect on and rate how they experienced receiving each type of prompt. Figure 3 shows the distribution of their responses on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from very
annoying (1) to totally fine (5). Very few participants rated either
prompt as very annoying, with the app-entry prompts receiving a
slightly higher proportion of such ratings than the general end-ofday prompts. Reflecting this general lack of user frustration, P16
(male, age 17) said of the app-entry prompts: “Having that little bit
of extra friction was not. . .it wasn’t as annoying as I thought it was
gonna be. And actually, it was more helpful too, in that it made me
reflect.”
The frequency of the prompts and the ability to dismiss them
without recording a response both appeared to play a role in minimizing user frustration, which is critical to the acceptability of
the intervention. Participants tended to agree that the number of
prompts they received each day did not feel onerous. Some participants cautioned that increasing the number of prompts (both
app-entry and end-of-day) may lead to people abandoning the app
out of frustration. Others felt that increasing the frequency slightly
would encourage people to reflect more on their social media use.
P61 (male, age 16) addressed this tension: “I feel like if you asked a

328

question every single time [you opened a social media app], it’d be
annoying, but also, it would make you think about [your goals for
social media] every single time.”
Several participants appreciated that responding to the prompts
was optional. P35 (female, age 17) observed: “The app is fairly noninvasive. . .it doesn’t block you every time you get a notification. I
think that would have been too invasive for me. . .but it was enough
so that you’re still aware of things.” However, some participants still
felt compelled to record a response even though it was optional.
P61(male, age 16) explained:
“Sometimes it was slightly annoying to use because I
just needed to go, get into the app. . .I was like, ‘I don’t
really wanna answer the question right now.’ But I was
kinda forced to either do it now or it was like, not answer
at all.”
This participant started to experience the prompts as a “barrier
to entry.” He suggested that he would have liked the option to
postpone answering a prompt until a time when he was in the right
frame of mind rather than have the prompt disappear altogether.
Several other participants similarly reflected that they would like
the ability to customize the app, such as by controlling the timing of
the prompts, deciding when to answer them, selecting the prompts
they liked best, adding their own prompts, and being shown their
responses over time.

RQ2 – Engagement in Self-Regulation
Behaviors Related to Social Media Use

analyzed their responses to interview questions about whether and

social media. We also analyzed responses to a Likert-scale question
included on the post-survey that asked participants to rate how
much they agreed or disagreed that Locus helped them to think
about their goals for using social media.
81% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that Locus helped
them to think about their goals for using social media (see Figure 4).
P16 (male, age 17) observed: “[Locus] helps me to. . .think about what

RQ3 – Perceived Impact on Teens’ Sense of
Control and Agency in their Social Media
Experiences

To examine evidence of the intervention’s potential effectiveness, we conducted paired-sample t-tests to compare levels of our
three outcome measures before and after the two-week intervention. Shapiro tests indicated that none of the measures violated
the assumption of normality. Figure 5 shows the pre- and postdeployment distributions, means, and confidence intervals for the
three measures: perceived self-control (BSCS), absentmindedness
(SUQ-A), and autonomy (TENS-Life) in relation to social media use.
For all three measures, there was a statistically significant difference between condition means. On average, participants reported
increased self-control (t(52 )=5.79, p< 0.001), decreased absentmindedness (t(52 )=2.74, p<0.01), and increased autonomy (t(52)=5.12,
p<0.001) in relation to their social media use from pre- to postdeployment. Table 2 summarizes these results, including effect
sizes.
In addition, 74% of participants agreed or strongly agreed with
the statement “The Locus app helped me feel more in control of my
social media use” (Figure 4). Taken together, these results provide
preliminary evidence that the Locus intervention had a positive
impact, at least in the short-term, on participants’ perceived selfcontrol and agency in their social media use. In the remainder of
this section, we dig deeper into these aggregate trends by drawing
on participants’ individual interview responses.
For some participants, their perceived increased sense of control
came from being able to reduce the amount of time they were
spending on social media: “I learned basically self-control. I learned
that, okay, you would have to minimize time you spend on social
media and focus more on work and other things” (P58, female, age
17).

6 DISCUSSION
6.1 Extending Digital Well-Being Designs to an
Adolescent Population
Adolescents are an under-researched group in HCI interventions
that target digital well-being even though adolescents struggle
to control, find meaning, and experience agency in their technology use [60, 68, 81]. Our work provides preliminary evidence that

===== PDF PAGE 10 =====

IDC ’23, June 19–23, 2023, Chicago, IL, USA

Katie Davis et al.

interaction designs that prompt teens to engage in goal setting,
reflection, planning, and self-monitoring are an acceptable form
of in-the-moment intervention and can potentially help teens—at
least in the short-term—to use social media in a more intentional
way. Results from our open trial field deployment (N =54) showed
that, on average, teen participants reported perceived increased selfcontrol and autonomy and decreased absentmindedness in their
social media use over the two week-period of using Locus. The
results provide a basis for future work investigating adolescents’
long-term use of and response to digital interventions, such as
Locus, that provide adolescents with in-the-moment support for
self-regulating their social media use.
Our exploratory field deployment provided evidence supporting the initial steps in our theory of change (Figure 1). Interview
participants discussed how Locus (intervention) prompted them to
engage in self-regulation behaviors related to their social media use
(mechanism of change). Over the course of the study, participants
observed themselves engaging in less habitual social media use
(characterized by “mindless scrolling”), approaching social media
with greater intention, and experiencing more meaning in their
interactions (proximal outcome).
We would not expect—and therefore did not measure—any longterm changes in well-being (distal outcome) over the course of
just two weeks. Future work is needed to examine the long-term
appropriation of Locus, including changes to how youth engage
with the app over time, and whether it succeeds in influencing their
well-being. Such an investigation is outside the scope of the current
work, which focused on the initial design of the Locus intervention
and an exploratory investigation into its acceptability and potential
effectiveness with a group of adolescents.
The current study connects to and extends prior HCI research
on digital well-being (e.g., [29, 44, 48, 68, 77, 84, 86]). We applied
insights generated from existing work to a population that is distinctly positioned to benefit from design interventions targeting
digital well-being. Adolescence represents a sensitive period for
the development of self-regulation skills [51]; it is also a time of
intensive social media use, which many youth struggle to manage
in an intentional way [20, 81]. In light of these challenges, we see
an opportunity for HCI research to identify interaction designs
that help build the self-regulation skills and shape the social media
habits that adolescents will carry with them into adulthood. The
current study contributes insight into promising interaction designs
that support adolescents’ intentional social media use, as well as
the contexts and individual characteristics that shape how they are
taken up by specific adolescents.
The results of our deployment study suggest that the set of interaction designs employed by Locus (see Section 3.4 and Figure
2) are acceptable to adolescents and hold promise for supporting
intentional social media use in an adolescent population. Overall,
adolescent participants adopted and used the Locus app with little trouble or annoyance. There was a general consensus among
interview participants that the frequency of the prompts (one appentry prompt per app per day, one general, end-of-day prompt)
was not too great to be annoying. In light of prior work showing
high rates of user frustration and abandonment of self-control tools
[43, 46], striking a balance between user engagement and frustration is critical. The inclusion of a response entry option (as opposed

331

to requiring users to record a response to every prompt) succeeded
at engaging participants in the target self-regulation skills without
producing too much frustration due to the fact that it was optional.
Overall, it appeared that Locus came close to a “Goldilocks” level
of self-control support for most participants [46].

6.2

Accounting for Individual Variation when
Designing for Adolescent Digital Well-Being

We gained useful insight from the few participants who said they
found little value in using Locus. For instance, P61 (male, age 16)
observed that, rather than disrupting his social media habits, answering the app-entry prompts themselves became as mindless
and habitual as his social media interactions. Although a minority
view, this perspective points to the need for future work to explore
which adolescents specifically are most likely to benefit from design
interventions, such as Locus, that target self-regulation skills in the
context of social media use.
For example, prior research suggests that problematic social
media use may be higher among adolescent girls than boys [37].
Future work could explore the moderating role of demographic
differences such as gender, age, socioeconomic status, and race,
as well as the role of individual characteristics such as executive
function, personality type, levels of existing dissatisfaction with
social media, and motivation to change one’s social media patterns.
Even among the majority of participants who perceived benefits from using Locus, several of them identified changes they felt
would improve the user experience and the intervention’s effectiveness. Moreover, there was considerable variation in their feedback.
Some participants wanted more frequent prompts, others less. Some
wanted it to be mandatory to respond to the prompts, others said
responding already felt mandatory. Some participants wanted to be
able to customize the app, for instance, by selecting their favorite
prompts and deciding for themselves when to answer them. Several
participants offered ideas for new features, such as a visualization
tool that shows their responses to prompts over time.
Returning to the concept of a “Goldilocks” level of support [46],
these findings indicate considerable individual variation across
adolescents in the “just right” level of support that is most likely to
be effective in scaffolding their self-regulation behaviors. It is also
worth exploring designs that are tailored to the environments of
specific social media platforms (e.g., [86]) and the distinct ways that
adolescents engage with them. Whereas social comparison may
be a relevant target of intervention for platforms like Instagram,
the lure of auto-play may be a more relevant focus for sites like
YouTube and TikTok (see [20]). Therefore, we recommend further
design investigations before testing the long-term impacts of Locus
and other interventions targeting adolescents’ intentional social
media use.

CONCLUSION

Digital well-being is a rapidly growing area of research in HCI.
There has been relatively little work to date that addresses adolescents as a specific user group, despite the fact that many adolescents struggle to manage and find meaning in their digital media
experiences. In light of their developmental challenges with selfregulation, adolescents stand to benefit from interventions that are

designed for and with them from the start. Drawing on psychology
theory and co-design practices, we developed Locus, a mobile application that shapes users’ entry experiences into the social media
platforms on their phones and encourages end-of-day reflection
about their interactions. Results from an open trial field deployment
(N = 54; M age =16.2 years) showed that Locus was an acceptable
intervention for this group of adolescents and showed evidence
of potential effectiveness. Participants reported engaging in more
frequent self-regulation behaviors in relation to their social media
use and perceived increases in their self-control and autonomy
and decreases in their absentminded social media use. The results
from the current study point to opportunities for future research,
such as customization features that allow adolescents to tailor the
intervention for their individual needs.

SELECTION AND PARTICIPATION OF
CHILDREN

All procedures involving minor participants received approval from
our university’s institutional review board (IRB). We recruited participants through word of mouth, social media announcements and
advertisements, and our university’s research-participant pool. All
procedures were explained to potential participants prior to their
participation, including the voluntary nature of participation, the
ability to end participation at any point during the study, and the
procedures in place to assure the confidentiality of their participation. We shared this information with participants electronically,
and they indicated their assent by clicking on a button indicating
they understood the nature of the study and agreed to participate
in it. For those teens who participated in co-design sessions and
follow-up interviews in the deployment study, we shared study
information both electronically and verbally. The university IRB
designated the study minimal risk and waived the requirement for
parental consent.
