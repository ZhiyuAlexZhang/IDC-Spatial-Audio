\section{Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

The teens in our sessions met spatial audio twice, first as listeners given an arrangement, then as designers envisioning one. As listeners they had to find the effect, and finding it told them little about whether they wanted it. What the demonstration did give them was a material, the separation of sounds they could hear and locate, and a standard, that an arrangement is worth having only in relation to the listening they already had. Their proposals took up both. They specified what an arrangement should let a listener hear and from where, and, when the arrangement was shared, whose experience each part of it was for. We present the findings in that order: how teens encountered and evaluated the demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:hearing}), what they envisioned for their own listening (Section~\ref{sec:choosing}), and what they envisioned for listening with other people (Section~\ref{sec:arranging}). The order is ours; the proposals were not solutions to difficulties with the demonstration. Names are pseudonyms, and we refer to the design groups by the design they developed.

\subsection{Encountering and Evaluating Spatial Audio}
\label{sec:hearing}

Teens' responses to the demonstration separated two judgments: whether they noticed the spatial arrangement and whether they found anything desirable in it. The first took work, and the second did not follow from it.

\subsubsection{Finding the difference}

The difference between the two versions was not self-evident, and finding it took work. Six of the eight first-question stickies in one group opened with a hedge of the form \textit{``I didn't hear too much of a difference''}. % [HDS1 Artifacts; VERIFY that the eight stickies are eight participants]
 Amara reflected:

\begin{quote}
\textit{I was personally expecting more of a difference between the normal one and the spatial. There were some parts\ldots{} that I could hear only on my left ear and not in my right, and that was kind of cool. But you had to listen pretty carefully. And I actually went back to the first one to really see the differences, because initially, I was like, ``These literally sound exactly the same.''}
% [HDS.1, 37:32]
\end{quote}

The display mattered to what teens could find. Sam, a musician, closed his eyes and found that \textit{``without really being able to see the dots show up\ldots{} it was kind of hard to differentiate what was on the left and what was on the right''}. % [MDS1.3, 08:59--09:15]
 Mara wondered whether the difference she heard was \textit{``just a placebo effect from watching the video and the icons of the different music parts showing up''}. % [HDS.3, 08:20]
 When the facilitator replied, \textit{``I think that's not placebo, though. I think that's actually the spatial audio working''}, % [08:41]
 Mara did not drop her doubt so much as relocate it, proposing that \textit{``seeing the audio mapped out on the screen makes you more aware of it as you hear it''} % [08:49]
 before adding that \textit{``even without looking at the visual, I could definitely hear a difference\ldots{} it had more depth to it''}. % [09:02]


What the teens could report distinguishing were separations. They heard the parts of the arrangement apart where before they had heard \textit{``one straight thing of audio''}; % [MDS1.3, 15:05]
 a sticky read \textit{``you could tell who was singing''}; Mara heard \textit{``each instrument\ldots{} instead of it all blending together into one''}. % [HDS.3, 09:30]
 Teens established the effect for themselves by comparison, going back to the first version, and by triangulation, checking their ears against the display, and what the comparison yielded was a set of locations and separately audible lines rather than a different song. In the demonstration the teens' attention went to the spatial effect itself, to locating it and deciding whether it was there. In their own proposals, described in Section~\ref{sec:choosing}, the spatial arrangement would instead direct attention to something else.

\subsubsection{Deciding whether the difference was worth having}

Hearing the difference did not make it desirable. Teens who heard it did not, on that account, want it, and their reasons separate liking a presentation from wanting a change to their listening. Nadia reflected that \textit{``at the end of the day, it's still the same music with the same notes\ldots{} I'd still listen to either version''}, % [HDS.1, 45:16]
 and her group's own grouping of its stickies put seven under \textit{``Changed the Experience''} and two under \textit{``No Change in the Experience,''} several of the seven hedged. Appreciation, where it came, depended on what the listener brought to the song. One participant \textit{``saw the difference, but it just didn't change my overall feel,''} because \textit{``it's not a song I'm familiar with''}, % [MDS1.2, 04:32--04:52]
 whereas Amara, \textit{``someone who doesn't listen to K-pop,''} found that the spatial version \textit{``made me appreciate it more 'cause I was able to hear some of the unique sounds in it''}. % [HDS.1, 46:14]
 The same separation of parts left one listener unmoved and gave the other a way into an unfamiliar song. Separation changed where attention went; whether that counted as an improvement was decided by the listener's relation to the music, and the demonstration could not decide it for them.

Wanting the effect in everyday listening was a further step, and several teens declined it for reasons that had nothing to do with the demonstration. One participant, asked what feature would make listening on the bus or during homework more fun, answered, \textit{``I think it's already fun''}. % [MDS1.1, 12:16]
 Another, asked whether he would want spatial audio in games, said \textit{``Absolutely,''} then added, \textit{``Although I do have a headset that automatically does that\ldots{} I'm already doing this''}. % [MDS1.2, 16:01--16:09]
 Teens who had met spatial sound before judged the demonstration against it. Lena, who knew 8D audio, found it \textit{``sometimes\ldots{} a little bit too much''}, % [HDS.3, 21:56]
 and concerts were the reference that recurred most, where \textit{``you kind of get to feel it all around you''} % [MDS1.3, 14:54--15:05]
 and where the demonstration fell short of \textit{``the ground shaking''}. % [HDS.3, 28:59--29:56; VERIFY speaker]


Where teens did want spatial audio, they wanted it on terms. Zoe wanted \textit{``a setting or a feature rather than a physical object''}, % [HDS.1, 01:24:30]
 and Kat argued that it should come with the product, \textit{``'cause when you buy AirPods, noise cancellation is a feature in it''}. % [01:36:29--01:37:20]
 Lena wanted \textit{``a choice where you could opt into the 8D audio,''} kept \textit{``integrated with the original sound just so more people could play with it''}. % [HDS.3, 55:55--56:17]
 Three kinds of reason for declining run through these accounts. The listening was already adequate, the listener already had the effect, or the effect could be too much. Where wanting appeared, it was conditional on choice and on fit with products the teens already used. The teens judged the arrangement's value against listening they already had and the circumstances in which they would meet it. The proposals that follow took the separation as their material and the standard as their question, first for the listener's own hearing and then for listening shared with other people.

\subsection{Envisioning Spatial Audio Around Oneself}
\label{sec:choosing}

The separation the teens had searched for in the demonstration became, in their proposals, something to build with. They gave spatial arrangements a purpose by fixing a listener's orientation to the sounds, which way the listener's attention points and where the listener's ears are. The first kind of proposal used direction to serve a task the listener already had; the second treated the listener's own position as something a device could keep or offer. In both, the arrangement was for one listener, and what it added was judged by the activity it served.

\subsubsection{Directing attention to particular sounds}

Several proposals used spatial separation so that one sound could be followed among others, and in each the sound to be followed was already part of the listener's situation. Nadia recalled a museum audio guide where \textit{``the room is so big''} that she did not know \textit{``what it's talking about,''} and wanted \textit{``some sort of sense of direction of where it's talking about''}. % [HDS.1, 56:16]
 Dev imagined earbuds that follow the gaze: \textit{``you look at someone, then their voice would\ldots{} focus only on their voice, and if you look away, then it would kind of weaken''}. % [HDS.3, 52:39]
 Lena connected that idea to her grandmother's hearing aids, where \textit{``everything is kind of the equal amount of sound that she's getting just amplified''}. % [HDS.3, 53:24--54:04]
 In the wrap-up discussion, Sam told the room that in \textit{``a shooter game, if you hear someone behind you, you're gonna turn around''}. % [MDS1.3 wrap-up, 33:31]


Two different uses of direction sit inside these proposals. In the museum and the game, direction is an index. The spatial cue tells the listener where to look or turn, and its value is that the listener then does something in the world. In the gaze earbuds, direction is a selection. The device uses where the listener is looking to decide which voice to bring forward, and Lena's comparison names what the selection adds over a hearing aid, which gives \textit{``the equal amount of sound\ldots{} just amplified.''} Separation, on this account, is what lets a listener attend to one voice among several, and amplification alone cannot do it. In none of these proposals does the listener do anything new to the sound; the arrangement does the pointing.

\subsubsection{Capturing or trying a listening perspective}

Two groups treated the position from which sound is heard as something a device could capture or offer, and the two designs differ in whether the position is one the listener actually occupied. The hoodie began when the group chose a sweatshirt from its own list and an adult suggested combining it with a teammate's \textit{``spatial recorder''} idea. % [MDS2.3, 12:57]
 The purposes the group gave the recording were musical and personal. One participant reframed it around a singer who \textit{``had a good song idea, they could press something, and it would record\ldots{} and you can play it again with spatial audio\ldots{} so you don't forget what you were saying''}; % [MDS2.3, 13:19--13:37; VERIFY speaker, merged label]
 others gave it a concert \textit{``you don't want to always have a thing to relive''}. % [13:51; VERIFY wording against the recording]
 The mic placement followed from these purposes. One participant put the mics \textit{``on each ear so you can capture the same stuff in here''}, % [19:07]
 and Max added a second mic near the mouth so that the wearer could \textit{``choose which you like better. Do you like it from right out of your mouth, or do you like it how you hear it better?''} % [21:25]
 (Figure~\ref{fig:details}a). When an adult asked whether a friend could share the experience by putting the hoodie on, a participant answered, \textit{``This was how I experienced the concert''}. % [16:15--16:19]


Max's question draws a distinction the group had been working toward. A mic at the mouth keeps the song idea, the sound as it left the singer. Mics at the ears keep how the wearer heard the concert, the sound as it arrived, and only the second needs spatial capture. The group's answer to the sharing question extends the distinction to replay. What is passed to the friend is one person's hearing of an event, from the position that person occupied.

The practice app fixed perspective at the other end, at playback. It began with the facilitator's proposal to make an acoustic instrument spatial. % [MDS2.2, 06:16--07:44]
 Within that frame, Rosa proposed \textit{``two settings''}, % [16:51]
 one in which \textit{``you could hear it run around or something''} % [17:21]
 and one in which \textit{``you would sound like at a concert''}. % [18:12]
 The facilitator then proposed hearing yourself as a Carnegie Hall audience would and introduced room acoustics; % [18:17--20:18]
 Eli reduced the idea to \textit{``Big room, small room''} % [20:13]
 and proposed that the app \textit{``generate a 3D room. And then maybe you could place yourself anywhere in that room''}. % [21:13--21:19]
 The adult framing, hearing oneself as an audience would, is what the teens' additions elaborate, and they elaborate it in one direction. Rosa's settings separate hearing one's own sound from hearing others; Eli's rooms and placement make the acoustic and the position things the performer chooses. The positions on offer are ones nobody has occupied. The hoodie preserves a perspective the wearer had; the practice app lets a performer try perspectives they have not had, in rooms they may never play in. One is a record and the other a rehearsal, and both place the choice of perspective with the person whose music it is. The hoodie's sharing moment, the friend who puts it on, is where that one-listener choice begins to involve someone else.

\subsection{Envisioning Spatial Audio with Others}
\label{sec:arranging}

The standard the teens had applied to the demonstration, that an arrangement must earn its place against a listener's own situation, became a question of whose situation once the arrangement was shared. Fitting it meant deciding whose experience each part of it was for, and two kinds of proposal did this. The first used placement to make other people present, in a call or around a table, and carried a cost the teens noticed as they proposed it. The second shared a musical experience across a car or a room while deciding, seat by seat, who would receive which effect.

\subsubsection{Making other people present through sound}

Nadia began from a problem with group calls and reasoned through a proposal in a single turn:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Whenever me and my friends are on FaceTime, it's like everyone's talking over each other and stuff like that. And I thought a cool way for spatial audio to help that is if they could make it so that it sounds like they're coming from different areas of the room. So if I'm sitting at my desk, one of my friends is in front of me to the left, and one of them is in front of me to the right, so that it feels like they're in\ldots{} I guess it'd kind of be weird to feel that they're in my room with me, but it would make it feel like that and easier communication.}
% [HDS.1, 55:21--55:41]
\end{quote}

The placement proposed for intelligibility and the presence Nadia found \textit{``weird''} are two consequences of one arrangement. Placing each friend's voice at a point in the room would make the call easier to follow, and the same placement would make absent friends present in a room they are not in. The order in which the two occurred to her is itself informative. The benefit motivated the design; the presence appeared only once she imagined the arrangement from her own desk, with particular friends in particular places, and having noticed it she returned to the benefit. Nadia did not withdraw the proposal, and we read her hesitation as the recognition that a call arranged for intelligibility is also a call that brings people into one's room.

The table group built the same arrangement out and gave it rules. Ren's reason was presence from the start: \textit{``people my age video call so much. And if different people came from different sides, it would help with feeling like you're actually having a conversation''}. % [HDS.2, 28:54]
 The design let \textit{``people choose what spot to sit at the table in the FaceTime app, and then their voices will project from there''}. % [50:45]
 The premium features that followed came from teens and the facilitator in turn. Ben proposed a \textit{``Surround Sound wrapped''}, % [01:01:32]
 which Hana called \textit{``a crazy invasion of privacy''} and extended anyway; % [01:02:25]
 Hana proposed paying \textit{``a premium to claim that seat so nobody else can take it''}; % [01:03:03]
 the facilitator proposed making \textit{``the table longer and longer''} for a friend who \textit{``said something you didn't like''} and a \textit{``silent little cone''}; % [01:03:40, 01:04:18]
 and Hana added, \textit{``Or a timeout?''}, % [01:04:30]
 recorded on the board as \textit{``Time out of shame.''} Hana called the set \textit{``really fun social gimmicks''}, % [01:10:44]
 approvingly.

Taken together, the call and the table show what happens when a spatial arrangement is built for presence. Every feature the table group added acts on someone else. Claiming a seat removes a choice from another person, the longer table, the cone, and the timeout push a friend's voice away or silence it, and the \textit{``wrapped''} exposes a record of everyone's talk. The group treated these as play, and the play was about doing things to the people at the table. Nadia's \textit{``weird''} and Hana's \textit{``invasion of privacy''} mark the same recognition at two scales, that an arrangement which makes people present also makes them subject to what it allows, and in both cases the teens noted the cost and kept the design.

\subsubsection{Sharing an experience across different listeners}

The car group's design had a history inside the session. Seventeen minutes before the design activity, Lena had already raised what a spatial mix would do in a car: \textit{``if you're in a car and then you're hearing one part of the music in the passenger seat and then a person in the backseat's hearing just bass\ldots{} that's maybe cool for one of them\ldots{} I feel like it would be kind of weird in a car setting unless you were sitting in the right spot''}, % [HDS.3, 56:37]
 and she concluded that \textit{``headphones is really what's popping out at me. I don't think you can really notice the difference without having headphones on''}. % [56:57]
 When the facilitator (the first author) later asked whether the car system should use headphones at all, Lena answered, \textit{``Headphones in the car?''}, and another participant called the idea \textit{``insane''} and \textit{``unsafe''}. % [01:07:31--01:07:37; VERIFY speaker]
 The group placed loudspeakers in the roof and the headrests and asked that they be \textit{``discreet''}. % [01:07:40--01:09:13]
 When the facilitator asked whether Lena wanted her seat to shake, she said, \textit{``that sounds cool, but I don't know if I want to be driving on the interstate and all of a sudden the song comes on and I'm shaking''}. % [01:11:29]
 Asked again whether she wanted a woofer around her seat, she answered, \textit{``yeah, I do, but maybe not in my seat''}, % [01:12:04]
 and a teammate moved it to \textit{``the passengers only''}. % [01:12:08]
 Lena then returned to her earlier worry:

\begin{quote}
\textit{I like that there's speakers on the left side, the middle, and the right side. But if you're in the driver's seat and then you're in the passenger seat, the one in the middle\ldots{} you would ask which part maybe it's playing. So I wonder if maybe you could put two speakers in the middle so that the driver is having the same experience as the people in the rest of the car.}
% [HDS.3, 01:13:17--01:13:38]
\end{quote}

A teammate checked, \textit{``Oh, so it balances out?''}, % [01:13:56]
 and the facilitator's paraphrase, \textit{``a central speaker to make sure that everyone has a more similar experience''}, % [01:14:01]
 became the board's label, \textit{``A middle speaker to standardize experiences''} (Figure~\ref{fig:details}b).

Two rules organize this design, and they pull in different directions. The music is to be the same for everyone; the middle speakers exist so that the driver is \textit{``having the same experience as the people in the rest of the car,''} which resolves the unequal seats Lena had worried about before the car was drawn. The bodily effect is to be given by role; the woofer that Lena wanted, \textit{``yeah, I do,''} goes to the passengers because the driver's body is doing something else. Shared listening, in this design, does not mean that everyone receives every effect. It means that the parts of the experience that make it one experience are equalized and the parts that could endanger or distract are assigned.

A room offered the other answer. Owen worked through whether everyone in his room of speakers would hear the same thing, decided that \textit{``everyone probably wouldn't experience the same amount of sound,''} and proposed \textit{``doors for every person. Then there's speakers inside, and they have their own TV\ldots{} Alone. Well, soundproof walls''}. % [MDS2.1, 13:18--13:56]
 Ivy's sheet-music tool played back \textit{``into speakers on your ears, so then you can hear it. But then others can't hear it. So it doesn't disturb anybody''}. % [MDS2.1, 22:03--22:38]
 Owen and Lena started from the same observation, that a shared loudspeaker space gives people in different positions different sound, and reached opposite designs, Owen separating the listeners and Lena equalizing the space. Ivy's answer makes the separation ear-worn, which keeps one person's practice from reaching anyone else. The proposals ranged well beyond the headphone listening the teens had been given, and the loudspeaker designs are where the fitting problem appeared, because a loudspeaker arrangement exists for everyone in the space at once. The ear-worn designs, the earbuds, the hoodie, and Ivy's playback, kept the arrangement individual and so never had to answer it.
