\section{Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

The teens in our sessions met spatial audio twice, first as listeners given an arrangement, then as designers envisioning one. As listeners they had to find the effect, and finding it told them little about whether they wanted it. What the demonstration did give them was a material, the separation of sounds they could hear and locate, and a standard, that an arrangement is worth having only in relation to the listening they already had. Their proposals took up both, in terms of activities they already had. We present the findings in that order: how teens experienced and evaluated the demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:hearing}), what they envisioned for their own listening (Section~\ref{sec:choosing}), and what they envisioned for listening with other people (Section~\ref{sec:arranging}). The order is ours; the proposals were not solutions to difficulties with the demonstration. Names are pseudonyms.

\subsection{Experiencing and Evaluating Spatial Audio}
\label{sec:hearing}

Teens' responses to the demonstration separated two judgments, whether they noticed the spatial arrangement and whether they found anything desirable in it, and the second did not necessarily follow from the first.

\subsubsection{Hearing spatial audio took work}

The spatial version was not self-evidently different from the standard one, and the teens who heard the difference did so by working at it. Six of the eight first-question stickies in one group opened with a hedge of the form \textit{``I didn't hear too much of a difference''}. Amara reflected:

\begin{quote}
\textit{I was personally expecting more of a difference between the normal one and the spatial. There were some parts\ldots{} that I could hear only on my left ear and not in my right, and that was kind of cool. But you had to listen pretty carefully. And I actually went back to the first one to really see the differences, because initially, I was like, ``These literally sound exactly the same.''}
\end{quote}

The display mattered to what teens could find. Sam, a musician, closed his eyes and found it \textit{``kind of hard to differentiate what was on the left and what was on the right''}. Mara wondered whether what she heard was \textit{``just a placebo effect from watching the video and the icons of the different music parts showing up''}. When the facilitator replied, \textit{``I think that's not placebo, though. I think that's actually the spatial audio working''}, Mara relocated her doubt, proposing that \textit{``seeing the audio mapped out on the screen makes you more aware of it as you hear it''} before adding that even without the visual \textit{``it had more depth to it''}.

What the teens could report distinguishing were separations. They heard the parts of the arrangement apart where before they had heard \textit{``one straight thing of audio''}, and Mara heard \textit{``each instrument\ldots{} instead of it all blending together into one''}. Teens established the effect by replaying and by checking their ears against the display, and what they found was a set of locations and separately audible lines rather than a different song. In the demonstration, attention went to the effect itself; in the proposals described in Section~\ref{sec:choosing}, the arrangement would direct attention to something else.

\subsubsection{Hearing spatial audio did not mean wanting it}

Hearing the spatial version did not necessarily make it desirable, and the teens' reasons separate liking a presentation from wanting a change to their listening. Nadia reflected that \textit{``at the end of the day, it's still the same music with the same notes\ldots{} I'd still listen to either version''}, and her group's own sorting of its stickies put seven under \textit{``Changed the Experience''} and two under \textit{``No Change in the Experience,''} several of the seven hedged. Where appreciation came, it depended on what the listener brought to the song. One participant \textit{``saw the difference, but it just didn't change my overall feel,''} because \textit{``it's not a song I'm familiar with''}, whereas Amara, \textit{``someone who doesn't listen to K-pop,''} found that the spatial version \textit{``made me appreciate it more 'cause I was able to hear some of the unique sounds in it''}. Whether that separation counted as an improvement was decided by the listener's relation to the music, and the demonstration could not decide it for them.

Wanting the effect in everyday listening was a further step, and the teens who declined it gave reasons that had little to do with the demonstration. One participant, asked whether he would want spatial audio in games, said \textit{``Absolutely,''} then added, \textit{``Although I do have a headset that automatically does that\ldots{} I'm already doing this''}, an answer that describes adoption, not refusal. Teens who had met spatial audio before judged the demonstration against it, concerts where \textit{``you kind of get to feel it all around you''} and 8D audio.

Where teens did want spatial audio, they wanted it on terms. Kat argued that it should come with the product, \textit{``'cause when you buy AirPods, noise cancellation is a feature in it,''} before allowing \textit{``a premium 'cause it's a new thing''} with a free trial. Three kinds of reason for declining run through these accounts: the listening was already adequate, the listener already had the effect, or the effect could be too much. Wanting, where it appeared, was conditional on fit and on trying before paying. The teens judged the arrangement's value against listening they already had, and the proposals that follow took the separation as their material and that standard as their question.

\subsection{Envisioning Spatial Audio Around Oneself}
\label{sec:choosing}

In their own proposals, the separation the teens had searched for became something to build with, and spatial audio took three roles in what they built: a cue that pointed them toward a sound that mattered, a record of music they wanted to keep, and an environment that sound filled.

\subsubsection{Spatial audio as a cue}

Several proposals used spatial audio as a cue, a way for one sound to be followed among others, and in each the sound to be followed already existed in the listener's situation. Nadia recalled a museum audio guide where \textit{``the room is so big''} that she did not know \textit{``what it's talking about,''} and wanted \textit{``some sort of sense of direction of where it's talking about''}. Dev imagined earbuds that follow the gaze: \textit{``you look at someone, then their voice would\ldots{} focus only on their voice, and if you look away, then it would kind of weaken''}. In the wrap-up discussion, Sam told the room that in \textit{``a shooter game, if you hear someone behind you, you're gonna turn around''}.

The proposals differ in what direction does for the listener. In the museum and the game, direction is an index: the cue tells the listener where to look or turn. In the gaze earbuds, direction is a selection, since the device uses where the listener is looking to decide which voice to bring forward. Lena reasoned about that difference through her grandmother's hearing aids, where \textit{``everything is kind of the equal amount of sound that she's getting just amplified''}, which on her account is what the earbuds would improve on.

\subsubsection{Spatial audio as a record}

The music-focused proposals treated spatial audio as a record, sound captured from a chosen position so that it could be heard again later and elsewhere. The teens put such records to two different ends, preserving a musical experience as it happened and changing its conditions for another purpose. The hoodie began when an adult suggested combining the group's sweatshirt with a teammate's \textit{``spatial recorder''} idea. One participant reframed it around a singer who \textit{``had a good song idea, they could press something, and it would record\ldots{} and you can play it again with spatial audio\ldots{} so you don't forget what you were saying''}, and others around a concert worth reliving. One participant put the mics \textit{``on each ear so you can capture the same stuff in here''}, and Max added a second mic near the mouth so that the wearer could \textit{``choose which you like better. Do you like it from right out of your mouth, or do you like it how you hear it better?''} (Figure~\ref{fig:details}a). Max's question draws the distinction the group had been working toward. A mic at the mouth keeps the song idea, the sound as it left the singer; mics at the ears keep how the wearer heard the concert, and only the second needs spatial capture. When an adult asked whether a friend could share the experience by putting the hoodie on, a participant answered, \textit{``This was how I experienced the concert''}. What is kept, and what is passed on, is one person's hearing of an event from the position they occupied.

The practice app changed the conditions instead. It began with the facilitator's proposal to make an acoustic instrument spatial. Within that frame, Rosa proposed \textit{``two settings''}, one in which \textit{``you could hear it run around or something''} and one in which \textit{``you would sound like at a concert''}. After the facilitator proposed hearing oneself as a Carnegie Hall audience would and introduced room acoustics, Eli reduced the idea to \textit{``Big room, small room''} and proposed that the app \textit{``generate a 3D room. And then maybe you could place yourself anywhere in that room''}. The teens' additions elaborate the adult framing in one direction. Rosa's settings separate hearing one's own sound from hearing others, and Eli's rooms make the acoustic and the listening position things the performer chooses, perspectives the performer has not occupied. Ben extended an adult's room-acoustics idea the same way, to performers who \textit{``could soundproof yourself within the Zoom system''}, an application for other musicians where the hoodie and the practice settings were for the teens' own music. Across the cases, a spatial recording was a choice between keeping a moment as it was heard and hearing it under conditions it never had, and the teens gave each choice a musical reason. In every case, what they designed was something to take with them.

\subsubsection{Spatial audio as an environment}

A third set of proposals treated spatial audio as an environment, sound that fills the place the listener is in and reaches the body. Where the hoodie was meant to keep a concert, these proposals were meant to put one in the room, and nothing in them was recorded or carried anywhere. One sheet included a room \textit{``made of speakers''} so that sound \textit{``comes at you from everywhere''}; the hoodie group's list began with a couch so that when \textit{``there's a giant roar of a dinosaur behind you\ldots{} you can also feel it in your back''}. The references the teens used were remembered rather than designed, concerts where \textit{``you kind of get to feel it all around you''} and, for Lena, virtual reality games that \textit{``mimic a similar 3D audio vibe''}. The headphone demonstration did not meet this model, and the proposals supplied what it lacked, loudspeakers around the body and vibration through it.

The same teens also described where surrounding sound stopped being wanted, and they described it in two different registers. Lena found 8D audio \textit{``almost off-putting if you're walking down an alley at night''}, a concern about being enveloped while attending to a street; a sticky in the same group read, \textit{``Sometimes it feels too real that it's disturbing,''} a concern about realism itself. We report these as separate reservations. What the proposals and the reservations share is a standard: the teens wanted sound to reach them as a concert does, and judged any arrangement that did so by whether the place and the moment could bear it.

\subsection{Envisioning Spatial Audio with Others}
\label{sec:arranging}

The standard the teens had applied to the demonstration, that an arrangement must earn its place against a listener's own situation, became a question of whose situation once the arrangement was shared. Two kinds of proposal answered it, one that placed distant friends around a listener and one that fitted sound to the people in a car or a room.

\subsubsection{Being with friends at a distance}

Teens proposed placing friends' voices in space so that group calls could be followed and would feel like being together. Nadia began from a problem with group calls and reasoned through a proposal in a single turn:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Whenever me and my friends are on FaceTime, it's like everyone's talking over each other and stuff like that. And I thought a cool way for spatial audio to help that is if they could make it so that it sounds like they're coming from different areas of the room. So if I'm sitting at my desk, one of my friends is in front of me to the left, and one of them is in front of me to the right, so that it feels like they're in\ldots{} I guess it'd kind of be weird to feel that they're in my room with me, but it would make it feel like that and easier communication.}
\end{quote}

The placement proposed for intelligibility and the presence Nadia found \textit{``weird''} are two consequences of one arrangement. The benefit motivated the design; the presence appeared only once she imagined the arrangement from her own desk, with particular friends in particular places, and she returned to the benefit without withdrawing the proposal. We read her hesitation as the recognition that a call arranged for following is also a call that brings people into one's room.

The table group built the same arrangement into a design and developed it as a matter of choosing places and shaping who takes part. Ren's reason was presence, since \textit{``people my age video call so much''} and voices from different sides \textit{``would help with feeling like you're actually having a conversation''}. The design let \textit{``people choose what spot to sit at the table in the FaceTime app, and then their voices will project from there''}. The features the group then added turned a chosen place into a claim and the table into something used on a friend. After the facilitator suggested that the app could tell you \textit{``your friend always sits on your left side when y'all hang out''}, Hana proposed that \textit{``they could pay a premium to claim that seat so nobody else can take it''}; the facilitator proposed that \textit{``if your friend said something you didn't like, you could make the table longer and longer''}; Hana added, \textit{``Or a timeout?''}. A reservation surfaced here too, of a different kind from Nadia's. When Ben proposed a \textit{``Surround Sound wrapped''}, Hana observed that extending it to \textit{``what your friend said the most''} would be \textit{``a crazy invasion of privacy''}, and the facilitator set the question aside: \textit{``we're brainstorming right now. We don't have to think about the actual ethics yet''}. Nadia's hesitation concerned the presence of friends in her room; Hana's concerned what a shared arrangement would record about them. In both cases the teens noticed, before any adult did, that an arrangement for being with friends would also do something to the friends.

\subsubsection{Listening alongside other people}

Where an arrangement served several people at once, the teens fitted it to the people in the space in three ways, making the music comparable, differentiating effects by role, and separating playback, and each came with a stated purpose. The car group's design had a history inside the session: seventeen minutes before the design activity, Lena had said what a spatial mix would do in a car: \textit{``you're hearing one part of the music in the passenger seat and then a person in the backseat's hearing just bass\ldots{} that's maybe cool for one of them\ldots{} unless you were sitting in the right spot''}. When the facilitator (the first author) later asked whether the car system should use headphones at all, Lena answered, \textit{``Headphones in the car?''}, and another participant called the idea \textit{``unsafe''}. Asked whether she wanted her seat to shake, Lena did not want to be \textit{``driving on the interstate and all of a sudden the song comes on and I'm shaking''}; asked again about a woofer, she said, \textit{``yeah, I do, but maybe not in my seat''}, and a teammate moved it to \textit{``the passengers only''}. Lena then returned to her earlier worry:

\begin{quote}
\textit{I like that there's speakers on the left side, the middle, and the right side. But if you're in the driver's seat and then you're in the passenger seat, the one in the middle\ldots{} you would ask which part maybe it's playing. So I wonder if maybe you could put two speakers in the middle so that the driver is having the same experience as the people in the rest of the car.}
\end{quote}

The facilitator's paraphrase, \textit{``a more similar experience''}, became the board's label (Figure~\ref{fig:details}b). Two rules organize the design. The music is to be comparable for everyone; the middle speakers secure it and resolve the unequal seats Lena had worried about before the car was drawn. The bodily effect is to be given by role, so the woofer Lena wanted goes to the passengers because the driver's body is doing something else. Sharing the car, in this design, did not mean that everyone received every effect.

Two proposals fitted sound to a shared space by separating listeners instead. Owen decided that in his room of speakers \textit{``everyone probably wouldn't experience the same amount of sound''} and proposed \textit{``doors for every person\ldots{} Alone. Well, soundproof walls''}. Ivy's sheet-music tool played back \textit{``into speakers on your ears, so then you can hear it. But then others can't hear it. So it doesn't disturb anybody''}. Owen and Lena started from the same observation, that a shared loudspeaker space gives people in different positions different sound, and reached opposite designs, Owen separating the listeners and Lena equalizing the space. Ivy's reason was other people's hearing, and her ear-worn playback keeps her practice to herself. The purposes were as concrete as the arrangements, a driver who must not be shaken, a car that should hear the same song, a household that should not have to hear someone practice, and fitting sound to other people meant deciding, for each of them, which parts of the experience they would share.
